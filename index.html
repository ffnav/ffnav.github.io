How Far Can a Feed-Forward Map Take a Robot?

Predicted floors often connect. They are still in the wrong place.

Abstract

An image-navigation system can keep a graph of images, objects or pixels, or reconstruct once and plan in 3D. Feed-forward reconstruction makes the latter tempting: a video produces posed geometry in one pass. If that geometry is ready for navigation, the relative maps and learned controllers used by topological methods become unnecessary. We test this premise. We rasterise the predicted floor, join adjacent cells under a step-height rule and execute routes with perfect localisation on ObjectReact's HM3D split and a Unitree Go2. The floor graphs often connect and the local geometry looks plausible, yet planned routes miss the true navigable floor because the predicted trajectory places surfaces in the wrong physical coordinates. Floor IoU and route-local error can detect this misplacement. For Scal3R, reusing predicted depth at odometry poses turns many failed maps into drivable ones while connectivity stays unchanged. A camera-height check provides a scene-reference-free consistency test before the robot moves. Relative image and topological methods remain useful because each motion is conditioned on the current view. Current feed-forward maps are not yet reliable for direct metric planning. Training losses should penalise geometric misplacement along routes, and evaluation should measure the same failure.

Key ideas

What we do

  • We test whether feed-forward 3D maps can replace relative navigation maps by planning and executing on outputs from four frontends under perfect localisation.
  • We show that map connectivity misses navigation-critical placement errors by comparing predicted floors and planned routes with Habitat's navmesh.
  • We isolate pose error by rebuilding Scal3R maps from the same predicted depth at recorded odometry poses, improving closed-loop execution in simulation and on a Unitree Go2.
  • We quantify how camera-height consistency, field of view, frame spacing and robot width affect whether the predicted floor supplies a route.

Method

From frames to a drivable floor

The method on one episode, from frames to the floor the goal reaches. Plum is reached, green is mapped but unreached.

Build

Rasterise and connect

We rasterise the predicted floor into cells and join adjacent cells under a step-height rule. This gives a floor graph directly from the frontend's output, with no relative map and no learnt controller.

Drive

Plan and execute

We plan on that graph and execute the route with perfect localisation, on ObjectReact's HM3D split and on a Unitree Go2. The question is not whether a path exists in the map, but whether it lands on the floor the robot must drive.

The four cases

What P1–P4 mean

Each route is driven under four conditions. P1 and P2 use the same predicted depth and differ only in whose poses it is placed at, which is what isolates pose error from depth error. P3 and P4 are references: one map the robot built itself, and one method that carries no map at all. These labels appear in the figures below and in the supplementary videos.

Takeaway

Conclusion

The tested frontends often reconstruct plausible local surfaces and connect the mapping run's endpoints. The route still fails when pose error puts those surfaces at the wrong coordinates. Connectivity cannot detect that shift. A score against the true floor can detect it, but a robot does not have the true floor at deployment.

Scal3R shows how much of the failure comes from pose. Replacing its poses with exact recorded poses raises arrivals from 2 of 32 to 26 of 32, even though every graph connects under both pose sets. Synthetic drift removes this gain while reachable floor stays almost constant. The camera-height check catches gross scale errors before driving, but it does not certify a route.

Feed-forward reconstruction cannot yet replace relative navigation by being rasterised and planned on once. It may reconstruct the right surfaces at the wrong coordinates. Relative methods remain useful because each action is tied to the current view. Direct metric mapping needs route-placement losses and benchmarks that execute the resulting routes.
